1957: Sputnik 1 launches and ignites the computing race

On 4 October 1957 the Soviet Union placed Sputnik 1, a polished aluminium sphere the size of a beach ball, into Earth orbit. The 83-kilogram satellite beeped for three weeks, yet those simple radio pulses produced an immediate and lasting shock in the United States. Within months Congress created the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA, later DARPA), pouring unprecedented funds into computing, control systems and what would soon be called artificial intelligence.

The practical problems of spaceflight demanded machines that could navigate, predict trajectories and make decisions faster than any human. Early digital computers were pressed into service for orbital mechanics; new programming techniques and real-time control theory followed. ARPA’s Information Processing Techniques Office, established in the wake of Sputnik, became the single most important patron of AI research for the next two decades. Projects that grew directly from this funding stream included time-sharing systems, interactive graphics, early natural-language programs and the first autonomous mobile robots.

The intellectual lineage is clear. Ideas that had lived mainly in university seminars—symbolic logic, cybernetics, information theory—suddenly acquired national urgency and industrial scale. Researchers who had worked in relative isolation now found themselves collaborating across laboratories, sharing code and data in ways that prefigured today’s open research culture. The same institutional machinery later supported the ARPANET, the direct ancestor of the internet on which modern large-scale machine learning depends.

Today’s AI landscape still carries the imprint of that October evening. Massive compute clusters, autonomous navigation systems, and the very notion that machines should reason under uncertainty all trace part of their pedigree to the scramble that began when a small silver sphere crossed the night sky. Sputnik did not contain a single line of AI code, yet it opened the institutional and financial channels through which artificial intelligence grew from speculative philosophy into an engineering discipline.
