1749: Johann Wolfgang von Goethe is born

On 28 August 1749, in the free imperial city of Frankfurt am Main, Johann Wolfgang von Goethe was born. Celebrated as Germany’s supreme literary figure, Goethe was also a tireless investigator of nature and a dramatist of the human drive to create life and mind by artifice. Those twin preoccupations place him, unexpectedly yet firmly, on the long intellectual path that leads to artificial intelligence.

In Faust Part II the alchemist Wagner succeeds in producing a Homunculus—a luminous, articulate being grown inside a sealed glass phial. The creature possesses knowledge, desire and wit, yet has no body of its own and must negotiate the world while still unfinished. Goethe’s scene is not mere fantasy; it condenses centuries of speculation about whether intellect can be manufactured rather than born, and what moral and metaphysical costs such an act might carry.

Goethe’s scientific writings reinforced the same theme from another angle. His morphological studies sought generative archetypes—the famous Urphänomen—beneath the variety of living forms. This developmental, holistic outlook later echoed in systems theory, developmental psychology and cybernetics, disciplines that supplied key concepts to early AI and cognitive science. The notion that complex organisation might arise from simple formative principles remains audible in today’s generative models and evolutionary robotics.

The Homunculus motif travelled onward through Mary Shelley’s creature, Čapek’s robots and the androids of modern science fiction, becoming a cultural template for debates about artificial minds. When researchers now discuss embodiment, grounding or the gap between fluent language and genuine understanding, they are still circling questions Goethe staged two hundred years ago.

Contemporary large language models and experimental agents can feel like distant descendants of that glass-bound intelligence: eloquent, knowledgeable, yet curiously incomplete. Goethe’s birthday therefore offers more than literary nostalgia. It reminds us that the ambition to craft a mind is older than any computer, and that the ethical and philosophical tensions surrounding AI were already being rehearsed, with unmatched poetic force, in the eighteenth century.
