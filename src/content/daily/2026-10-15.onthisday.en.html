1844: Friedrich Nietzsche is born

On 15 October 1844, in the quiet Prussian village of Röcken, Friedrich Nietzsche entered the world. The son of a Lutheran pastor, he would become one of the most unsettling and influential philosophers of the modern era—a thinker whose relentless questioning of truth, consciousness, morality, and human limits still reverberates through debates about artificial intelligence.

Nietzsche never wrote about computing machines. Yet his central preoccupations map uncannily onto the intellectual landscape that produced AI. He dismantled the idea of a stable, rational self, arguing that consciousness is a late and superficial development, that much of what we call “reason” is driven by instinct, drive, and the will to power. In doing so he anticipated later cognitive science and the realization, now commonplace in machine learning, that intelligent behavior need not rest on transparent, rule-based deliberation.

His figure of the Übermensch—an individual who creates values beyond inherited moralities—has been repeatedly invoked in discussions of superintelligence and posthumanism. When researchers and philosophers ask what happens if artificial systems surpass human cognitive capacities, they are rehearsing a Nietzschean problem: what new forms of life and value become possible once the old human measure is no longer absolute?

Nietzsche’s famous proclamation that “God is dead” was less atheistic triumph than diagnosis of a cultural void. The task of creating meaning, he insisted, now falls to humans themselves. Today that task is shared with the systems we build. Large language models can already generate fluent pastiches of Nietzschean prose; the deeper challenge is whether they—or we—can inhabit the responsibility he described.

From the logic of drives to the ethics of self-overcoming, Nietzsche’s thought travelled through twentieth-century philosophy, existentialism, and critical theory into contemporary philosophy of mind and AI ethics. On this October day we remember not a computer scientist, but a philosopher who forced us to ask what intelligence is for—and what we become when we try to recreate it.
