1839: Charles Sanders Peirce is born

On 10 September 1839, Charles Sanders Peirce was born in Cambridge, Massachusetts. The son of a Harvard mathematician, Peirce grew into one of the most original American thinkers of the nineteenth century: a logician, philosopher, scientist and semiotician whose ideas quietly shaped the intellectual soil from which artificial intelligence would later grow.

Peirce extended George Boole’s algebra of logic into a full theory of relations. His “logic of relatives” treated predicates that link multiple objects—exactly the kind of structure later formalised in first-order predicate logic, the language still used to represent knowledge in many AI systems. He also invented existential graphs, a diagrammatic notation that anticipated modern visual and spatial logics. More practically, in the 1880s he described electrical switching circuits that could evaluate logical propositions, decades before Claude Shannon’s celebrated master’s thesis made the same connection the foundation of digital electronics.

Equally important for today’s AI is Peirce’s theory of inquiry. He distinguished three forms of reasoning: deduction, induction and abduction. Abduction—the imaginative leap that forms a plausible hypothesis to explain surprising data—is now recognised as a core process in machine learning, scientific discovery systems and large language models that generate candidate explanations. His semiotics, the study of signs and their interpretation, supplies a conceptual vocabulary for understanding how symbols acquire meaning inside both human minds and artificial agents.

Though Peirce died in relative obscurity in 1914, his manuscripts were gradually published and absorbed by later generations of logicians, computer scientists and cognitive researchers. From relational databases and knowledge graphs to probabilistic programming and neuro-symbolic architectures, traces of his thought remain visible. On this anniversary we remember a restless polymath who, long before electronic computers existed, was already mapping the logical and inferential pathways that artificial intelligence still travels.
