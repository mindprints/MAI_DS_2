1892: Louis de Broglie is born

On 15 August 1892, in the French coastal town of Dieppe, Louis Victor Pierre Raymond de Broglie entered the world. Born into an aristocratic family, he first trained as a historian before the mysteries of physics claimed him. The path he then took would supply one of the essential scientific pillars on which computing—and therefore artificial intelligence—rests.

In his 1924 doctoral thesis de Broglie advanced a startling hypothesis: that every particle of matter is also a wave. Electrons, he argued, possess a wavelength inversely proportional to their momentum. The idea seemed almost philosophical, yet experiments quickly confirmed electron diffraction. Wave–particle duality became a founding principle of quantum mechanics, earning de Broglie the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1929.

Quantum mechanics, in turn, explained the behaviour of electrons in crystalline solids. That understanding enabled the invention of the transistor at Bell Labs in 1947 and, soon afterwards, the integrated circuit. Every microprocessor, every graphics processing unit that trains today’s neural networks, is a direct technological descendant of de Broglie’s insight.

The connection does not stop at hardware. Contemporary research in quantum machine learning seeks to harness superposition and entanglement for computational advantages that classical machines cannot match. Even debates about the physical basis of mind occasionally return to quantum effects, keeping de Broglie’s intellectual descendants active at the frontier of AI theory.

Thus an August birthday in nineteenth-century France quietly echoes in the server farms and research labs of the twenty-first century. When we speak of artificial intelligence, we are also speaking, however indirectly, of the elegant waves that Louis de Broglie taught us to see in ordinary matter.
