1654: Blaise Pascal experiences his Night of Fire

On 23 November 1654 the French mathematician, physicist and philosopher Blaise Pascal lived through a two-hour mystical experience he later called the Night of Fire. He immediately recorded the event on a scrap of parchment—the famous Memorial—that he kept sewn into the lining of his coat for the rest of his life. While the night itself was intensely religious, it belongs to a career already dense with inventions and arguments that would travel, centuries later, into the foundations of artificial intelligence.

A decade earlier Pascal had built the Pascaline, a brass mechanical calculator that performed addition and subtraction by means of rotating dials and carry mechanisms. It was one of the first true digital calculating machines, conceived to spare his father the drudgery of tax arithmetic. In the same year as the Night of Fire he and Pierre de Fermat exchanged the letters that founded modern probability theory, turning questions about dice games into a rigorous calculus of chance.

Those two strands—automated arithmetic and the mathematics of uncertainty—proved decisive. Mechanical calculation evolved through Babbage, Lovelace, Hollerith and the electronic computers of the 1940s; probability theory became the language of statistical inference, information theory, Bayesian networks and virtually every contemporary learning algorithm. Pascal’s insistence that a machine could reliably manipulate symbols prefigured the central claim of AI: that intelligence itself might be rendered as computation.

Today the same ideas surface in large-scale machine learning. Every neural-network training run rests on probabilistic loss functions and stochastic gradient descent; every reliable arithmetic unit inside a GPU traces a distant line back to the Pascaline’s geared wheels. The Night of Fire did not create artificial intelligence, yet it marks a moment when one of the tradition’s earliest architects paused, reflected, and left a record. On this day we remember how the dream of thinking machines began with a young man, a candle-lit room, and a handful of ideas about number, chance and mechanism.
