1694: Voltaire is born

On 21 November 1694, François-Marie Arouet—better known by his pen name Voltaire—was born in Paris. A leading voice of the Enlightenment, he spent his life championing reason, wit, and the critical examination of received ideas. His writings helped shape the intellectual climate in which later thinkers would try to mechanize thought itself.

Voltaire’s most famous collision with the prehistory of artificial intelligence came through his satire of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. In the 1759 novella Candide, he mercilessly mocked Leibniz’s claim that we live in “the best of all possible worlds.” Yet the same Leibniz had earlier dreamed of a characteristica universalis and a calculus ratiocinator: a universal logical language and a calculating method that would settle philosophical disputes by computation rather than rhetoric. “Let us calculate” was to replace “let us argue.”

That dream travelled a long road. Leibniz’s own work on binary arithmetic supplied a key ingredient. George Boole turned logic into algebra; Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell gave it precise symbolic form; Alan Turing and others showed how such formal systems could be embodied in machines. The symbolic and logicist traditions of early AI are direct descendants of the Leibnizian project that Voltaire lampooned.

Voltaire’s skepticism remains useful. He never trusted pure reason untempered by experience and irony. Today’s large language models and automated reasoners inherit both halves of the Enlightenment legacy: the ambition to calculate thought, and the reminder that elegant systems can still produce absurd or harmful results. When researchers debate alignment, hallucination, or the limits of formalization, they are, in a sense, still arguing with Candide—and with the optimistic rationalist he so cheerfully dismantled.

Thus a Parisian birth on a November day in 1694 marks one more strand in the long braid of ideas that became artificial intelligence: the conviction that reason can be made mechanical, forever shadowed by the suspicion that it never quite can.
