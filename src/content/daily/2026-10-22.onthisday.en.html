1938: Chester Carlson makes the first xerographic copy

On 22 October 1938, in a rented room above a bar in Astoria, Queens, a patent attorney named Chester Carlson and his assistant Otto Kornei produced the world's first xerographic image. They wrote "10-22-38 ASTORIA" in India ink on a glass microscope slide, dusted a sulphur-coated zinc plate with lycopodium powder after charging it with static electricity and light, and transferred the resulting image onto wax paper. It was crude, but it worked: an image copied not by photography's chemistry, but by electrostatics.

Carlson's "electrophotography" had little to do with minds, machines that think, or logic gates. Yet the institution it eventually created would shape the hardware and culture of artificial intelligence research for decades. Carlson spent six years failing to interest companies in his invention before the Haloid Company licensed it in 1947. Haloid later renamed itself Xerox, and the extraordinary profits from plain-paper copiers let Xerox fund a research centre unlike any other.

That centre, Xerox PARC, opened in Palo Alto in 1970. Within a decade its researchers had built the Alto, the first computer with a graphical desktop, overlapping windows, and a mouse; invented Ethernet networking; prototyped laser printing; and created Smalltalk, one of the first object-oriented programming languages. None of this was "AI" in the narrow sense, but it built the environment in which AI would later be developed, taught, and used — the windows, icons, pointers, and networks that let researchers write, share, and run the code behind expert systems, neural networks, and today's large language models.

There is a second, quieter thread too. Carlson's machine solved, by chemistry and electrostatics, a problem that computer vision and optical character recognition would later try to solve computationally: how to take a fixed mark on paper and reproduce or interpret it reliably. Long before any algorithm could "read" a page, the photocopier had already learned, after a fashion, to see one.

So the office photocopier, that most unglamorous of machines, has a genuine place in AI's family tree — not as an ancestor of its ideas, but as part of the industrial and technical infrastructure that made the idea-makers' work possible. When today's AI tools run in windows, on networks, through interfaces descended from PARC's Alto, they are drawing, however indirectly, on the fortune that a patent lawyer's static-electricity experiment, in a rented room in Astoria in October 1938, helped build.
