1860: Elmer Sperry, pioneer of automatic control, is born

On 12 October 1860, Elmer Ambrose Sperry entered the world in Cortland, New York. Few at the time could have guessed that this inventor’s practical machines would help lay groundwork for the science of self-regulating systems—and, eventually, for artificial intelligence.

Sperry became one of America’s most prolific inventors, holding more than 400 patents. His greatest contributions centered on the gyroscope. He developed the gyrocompass that freed ships from magnetic interference, gyroscopic stabilizers for ships and aircraft, and early autopilots. These devices continuously measured a system’s state, compared it with a desired goal, and applied corrective action—precisely the feedback loop that later theorists would formalize.

In the 1940s Norbert Wiener and others forged the new field of cybernetics by studying exactly such control and communication in animals and machines. Wiener openly drew on the engineering tradition of automatic governors, servomechanisms and gyroscopic instruments that Sperry had turned into reliable technology. Cybernetics supplied early AI researchers with core ideas: goal-seeking behavior, information as a quantity, and the possibility that mental processes could be understood as feedback circuits.

From those mid-century conversations the thread runs straight into the present. Modern robotics still depends on the same closed-loop control Sperry engineered; reinforcement-learning agents adjust their actions through continuous feedback; even large language models are steered by reward signals and iterative refinement. Whenever an autonomous drone holds a steady hover or a neural network corrects its own predictions, it is performing a sophisticated descendant of the self-correcting dance Sperry first taught to steel and spinning wheels.

On this October day we therefore remember not only an ingenious engineer but a quiet ancestor of the intelligent machines now taking shape around us.
