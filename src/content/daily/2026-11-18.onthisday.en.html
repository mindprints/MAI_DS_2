1962: Niels Bohr dies

On 18 November 1962 the Danish physicist Niels Henrik David Bohr died in Copenhagen at the age of 77. Already a Nobel laureate and one of the central architects of quantum mechanics, Bohr left behind a body of work that quietly underpins every digital computer—and therefore every modern AI system—running today.

In 1913, while still in his twenties, Bohr proposed a radical model of the atom: electrons travel in discrete orbits and can jump between them only by absorbing or emitting fixed quanta of energy. The model explained the spectral lines of hydrogen and marked a clean break with classical physics. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1922. In the decades that followed he refined the Copenhagen interpretation and formulated the principle of complementarity—the idea that certain pairs of properties cannot be known simultaneously with arbitrary precision—while his institute became a magnet for Heisenberg, Pauli, Dirac and many others.

The path from Bohr’s atom to artificial intelligence runs straight through hardware. Transistors, the switches inside every processor, operate according to the quantum behaviour of electrons in semiconductors. Band gaps, tunnelling and Fermi–Dirac statistics are direct descendants of the quantum framework Bohr helped build. Without that framework there would be no integrated circuits, no GPUs, and no practical way to perform the billions of parallel operations required to train today’s neural networks.

Bohr’s more philosophical reflections travelled a parallel route. His insistence that the act of observation is inseparable from what is observed later resonated in cybernetics, cognitive science and debates about machine knowledge. Those same questions reappear whenever researchers try to interpret a deep network’s decisions or explore whether quantum algorithms might one day accelerate machine learning itself.

Thus a physicist’s death in a quiet Copenhagen suburb more than sixty years ago still echoes in the data centres and laboratories where artificial intelligence is built. The silicon is quantum; the questions remain Bohr’s.
