1852: Ada Lovelace dies

On 27 November 1852, Augusta Ada King, Countess of Lovelace, died in London at the age of thirty-six. The daughter of the poet Lord Byron and the mathematically gifted Annabella Milbanke, Ada had been steered toward science and numbers from childhood as a deliberate counterweight to what her mother feared was a dangerous poetic inheritance. That education prepared her for one of the most consequential collaborations in the prehistory of computing.

In the 1840s she worked closely with Charles Babbage on his proposed Analytical Engine. When she translated an Italian engineer’s article about the machine, she added a set of extensive “Notes” that more than doubled the original length. In those Notes she described, with striking clarity, how the Engine might manipulate symbols according to rules, not merely crunch numbers. She sketched what is now recognized as the first published computer algorithm and imagined the machine composing music or producing graphics—activities that required representing and transforming abstract information.

Lovelace was careful to insist that the Engine could not originate anything on its own; it could do only what it was instructed to do. Yet the very act of separating the machine’s operations from pure arithmetic opened a conceptual door. If symbols could stand for anything—notes, logical propositions, later still neural activations—then a general-purpose engine became conceivable. That insight travelled slowly. Babbage’s mechanical designs were never fully built, and her Notes languished in relative obscurity for decades.

In the twentieth century the thread was picked up again. Historians and computer scientists rediscovered her work; Alan Turing cited the possibility of machines performing tasks once thought uniquely human; and the architects of electronic computers found in her writings an early articulation of software as distinct from hardware. Today’s large language models, image generators and planning systems are distant descendants of the same idea: that a machine following formal rules can operate on representations of the world far richer than numbers alone.

On the anniversary of her death it is worth remembering that the intellectual path to artificial intelligence did not begin in mid-twentieth-century laboratories. It began, in part, with a young mathematician who saw further into a machine that existed only on paper than most of her contemporaries could see into the devices they actually held.
