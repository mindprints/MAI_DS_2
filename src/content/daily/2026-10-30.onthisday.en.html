1963: Clifford Berry, co-builder of the first electronic digital computer, dies

On 30 October 1963, Clifford Berry was found dead in his apartment in Huntington, New York, at the age of 45. His death was ruled a suicide, though questions about the circumstances have never fully disappeared. Today he is remembered, when he is remembered at all, as the overlooked half of a partnership that quietly reshaped computing.

Between 1939 and 1942, Berry worked alongside physicist John Vincent Atanasoff at Iowa State College to build what historians now call the Atanasoff–Berry Computer, or ABC. It was not programmable in the modern sense — it was built to solve systems of linear equations — but it introduced ideas that would become foundational: binary representation of numbers, electronic switching with vacuum tubes instead of mechanical relays, and a regenerative capacitor memory that refreshed its own charge, an ancestor of today's dynamic RAM. Berry, the engineer of the pair, did much of the hands-on design and construction that turned Atanasoff's concepts into working hardware.

The ABC never became famous in its own time. It was disassembled for parts during the war, and for decades the invention of the electronic digital computer was credited to the much larger, much later ENIAC, built at the University of Pennsylvania. Only in 1973, a decade after Berry's death, did a US federal court rule in Honeywell v. Sperry Rand that ENIAC's patent was invalid, finding that its designers had derived key ideas from Atanasoff — a quiet vindication that Berry did not live to see.

This obscure Iowa laboratory sits at the root of a long chain that runs through ENIAC, the transistor, the integrated circuit, and eventually the GPUs that train today's large language models. Every one of them still depends, at bottom, on the same move Berry helped pioneer: representing information as binary states and switching them electronically rather than mechanically. The dream of a thinking machine is often told through famous names — Turing, von Neumann, McCarthy — but it rests just as much on engineers like Berry, whose careful soldering and wiring made the abstractions of logic and mathematics physically real.

The Museum of Artificial Intelligence remembers Clifford Berry not for a triumphant demonstration, but as a reminder: behind every "intelligent" system humming in a data centre today lies a long, often uncredited history of people who built the machines that made thinking machines possible.
