1843: Hamilton discovers quaternions on Brougham Bridge

On the evening of 16 October 1843, the Irish mathematician William Rowan Hamilton was walking with his wife along the towpath of the Royal Canal in Dublin. For more than a decade he had tried to extend the two-dimensional arithmetic of complex numbers into three dimensions. As they neared Brougham Bridge the solution arrived in a sudden flash: four dimensions were required, not three. In his excitement Hamilton pulled out a pocket knife and carved the fundamental relations into the stone of the bridge: i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1.

The entities he named quaternions comprise one real part and three imaginary parts. They form a non-commutative division algebra—the first of its kind. Hamilton devoted the remainder of his career to their theory, certain that they held deep significance for geometry and mechanics.

After his death quaternions were largely eclipsed by the simpler vector calculus of Gibbs and Heaviside. Yet their unmatched capacity to encode a rotation in three-dimensional space without singularities kept the idea alive among a small circle of specialists.

With the advent of digital computers that capacity became indispensable. Engineers building early flight simulators and robot controllers rediscovered that unit quaternions supply compact, numerically stable, and gimbal-lock-free orientation control. The same mathematics now sits at the core of many artificial-intelligence systems that must reason about the physical world.

Today quaternions appear in:

  • attitude estimation filters that keep drones and autonomous vehicles stable,
  • pose-estimation networks that enable robot grasping and manipulation,
  • camera-path interpolation inside neural radiance fields and 3-D generative models,
  • rotation-equivariant layers of geometric deep learning.

Every time an AI system calculates how an object is turned in space, it quietly draws on the insight Hamilton scratched into a Dublin bridge on this day in 1843.
