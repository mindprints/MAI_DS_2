2011: John McCarthy, who named "Artificial Intelligence," dies

On 24 October 2011, John McCarthy died at his home in Stanford, California, at the age of 84. Few single deaths in the history of computing carry such a direct link to the field this museum is devoted to: McCarthy did not merely contribute to artificial intelligence, he gave it its name.

Born in Boston in 1927 to immigrant parents, McCarthy showed an early gift for mathematics, working through a Caltech calculus textbook as a teenager before formally enrolling there. By the mid-1950s, as a young mathematician at Dartmouth College, he had become convinced that "every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it." That sentence, from the 1955 proposal he co-authored for a summer workshop, introduced the phrase "artificial intelligence" to the world. The 1956 Dartmouth workshop it funded is now conventionally treated as the field's founding event.

McCarthy's own technical legacy runs just as deep. In 1958 he designed LISP, a programming language built around symbolic lists and recursive functions rather than numerical computation. LISP became the lingua franca of AI research for decades, and its innovations — garbage collection, conditional expressions, functions as first-class objects — quietly reshaped programming languages far beyond AI. He also pioneered time-sharing, the idea that many users could interact with one computer simultaneously, a precondition for the kind of responsive computing we now take for granted.

Philosophically, McCarthy championed a logicist vision of intelligence: that reasoning, including common sense, could be captured through formal logic and explicit representation of knowledge. His 1959 paper "Programs with Common Sense" proposed an "advice taker" system that could draw conclusions from stated facts — an ambition that has resurfaced, in new clothing, in today's debates about whether large language models truly reason or merely pattern-match.

McCarthy received the Turing Award in 1971 and remained intellectually active until his death, still arguing for formal, knowledge-based approaches even as statistical machine learning rose to dominance. Today's AI landscape, built largely on neural networks and vast datasets, might look unfamiliar to him — yet the very term we use to describe it, and the question of what genuine reasoning requires, both trace back to the man who died on this day.

  • 1955: Co-authors the Dartmouth proposal, coining "artificial intelligence"
  • 1958: Invents the LISP programming language
  • 1959: Publishes "Programs with Common Sense"
  • 1971: Awarded the Turing Award
  • 24 October 2011: Dies in Stanford, California
