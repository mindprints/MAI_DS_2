1815: George Boole is born

On 2 November 1815, George Boole was born in Lincoln, England, the son of a shoemaker with a passion for mathematics and optics. Boole had almost no formal higher education — he was largely self-taught, working as a schoolteacher while devouring the works of Newton, Lagrange, and Laplace in his spare time. Nothing about his modest beginnings suggested that his ideas would one day underpin every digital device on Earth.

Boole's great insight, developed over decades and crystallised in his 1854 book An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, was that logical reasoning could be treated as a form of algebra. Statements could be true or false, represented as 1 or 0, and combined using operations we now call AND, OR, and NOT. What had been the domain of philosophers since Aristotle became, in Boole's hands, a system of calculation — a way of computing with logic itself.

For decades, Boolean algebra remained a mathematical curiosity, admired by logicians but with no obvious practical use. That changed in 1937, when a young MIT graduate student named Claude Shannon realised that Boole's true/false algebra mapped perfectly onto electrical switching circuits: a switch is either open or closed, just as a proposition is either true or false. Shannon's master's thesis showed how any logical function could be built from relays and switches, giving engineers a mathematical language for designing circuits — the blueprint for every computer built since.

The thread from Boole's quiet study in Lincoln to today's AI systems runs remarkably straight:

  • Boole's algebra of logic became the theoretical foundation of circuit design;
  • Shannon's application of it gave rise to digital computing hardware;
  • logic gates built on Boolean principles still form the physical substrate of every processor, including those training and running today's neural networks;
  • Boole's dream of mechanising thought also echoes in symbolic AI and logic programming, even as the field moved toward statistical and neural approaches.

It is a fitting irony that a man whose work was once dismissed as abstract philosophy now underlies the silicon powering large language models and machine learning systems. Every time a transistor switches on or off inside a modern AI chip, it is quietly performing the algebra George Boole imagined in the nineteenth century as a way of capturing the laws of human thought.
