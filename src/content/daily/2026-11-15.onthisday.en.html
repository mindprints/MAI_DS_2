1971: Intel releases the 4004 microprocessor

On 15 November 1971, Intel Corporation publicly introduced the 4004, the world’s first commercially available microprocessor. The chip measured only a few millimetres across, held 2,300 transistors and could execute roughly 60,000 operations per second. Designed by Federico Faggin, Ted Hoff and Stan Mazor originally for a Japanese calculator manufacturer, it placed the essential functions of a central processing unit on a single piece of silicon.

Until that moment, electronic computers were built from racks of discrete components or several larger integrated circuits. The notion of a “computer on a chip” instantly changed the economics and scale of electronics. Calculators shrank, embedded controllers became practical, and the path opened to the affordable personal computers that appeared later in the decade.

The 4004 did not arise in isolation. Its intellectual ancestry runs from Charles Babbage’s Analytical Engine and Ada Lovelace’s notes on programming, through Alan Turing’s abstract model of computation and the wartime engineering of machines such as ENIAC, to Claude Shannon’s switching theory and the invention of the transistor and the integrated circuit. Integrating an entire CPU was simply the next logical compression of that long lineage.

In the half-century since, transistor counts have grown by factors of millions, following the trajectory popularly known as Moore’s Law. The resulting abundance of inexpensive computation is what makes contemporary artificial intelligence feasible: the training of large neural networks, real-time inference on edge devices, and the vast data-centre clusters that host today’s language models all rest on the microprocessor foundation laid in 1971.

A modest calculator chip ordered for a business machine thus became an unnoticed hinge in the history that leads to modern AI.
