1833: Alfred Nobel is born

On 21 October 1833, Alfred Nobel was born in Stockholm, the son of an engineer and inventor. He would go on to patent dynamite, build an industrial empire spanning explosives and armaments, and, in a will signed in 1895, endow the prizes that bear his name. None of this has anything to do with artificial intelligence — and yet, more than a century later, the institution Nobel created would become an unlikely marker of AI's arrival as a mature science.

Nobel's five original prize categories — Physics, Chemistry, Physiology or Medicine, Literature, and Peace — reflected the shape of knowledge in his own time. There was no category for mathematics, logic, or the emerging science of computation, fields that would later supply the intellectual scaffolding of AI: Boolean algebra, Turing's theory of computability, Shannon's information theory, McCulloch and Pitts' formal neurons. For most of the twentieth century, the people building these ideas simply worked outside the Nobel's five windows.

That finally changed in October 2024, when the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics jointly to John Hopfield and Geoffrey Hinton, "for foundational discoveries and inventions that enable machine learning with artificial neural networks." The citation recognised:

  • Hopfield's 1982 associative-memory network, which showed how a system of simple units could store and retrieve patterns using ideas borrowed from statistical physics;
  • Hinton's work on the Boltzmann machine and on backpropagation, which turned those physics-inspired networks into trainable learning systems and helped launch the deep learning era.

It was a striking moment: a prize rooted in nineteenth-century physics, bearing the name of a man who never imagined computers, being used to honour the mathematical physics of memory and learning that now underlies large language models, image recognisers, and recommendation engines. The committee itself noted the irony, stressing that the work being honoured was physics, even though its consequences are everywhere in software.

Alfred Nobel's birthday is a reminder that the categories we use to organise knowledge are historical accidents, not natural boundaries. Artificial intelligence grew up stitched together from logic, neuroscience, statistics, and engineering precisely because it did not fit into any single existing discipline. A century and a half after Nobel's birth, the prize he endowed finally had to stretch to accommodate it — a small sign of how thoroughly AI has reshaped the map of the sciences it was born from.
