1466: Desiderius Erasmus is born

On 27 October 1466 (the traditional date; his birth year is disputed), Desiderius Erasmus was born in Rotterdam. The illegitimate son of a priest and a physician’s daughter, he overcame early hardship to become the foremost scholar of the Northern Renaissance and the defining voice of Christian humanism.

Erasmus championed education, critical scholarship and the dignity of human reason. His satire The Praise of Folly (1511) ridiculed dogma and hypocrisy, while his scholarly editions of classical and biblical texts insisted on returning to original sources. In 1524 he published De libero arbitrio, defending free will against Martin Luther’s doctrine of absolute predestination. The debate placed human agency and moral responsibility at the centre of intellectual life.

That humanist faith in the improvability of reason travelled through the centuries. Leibniz, an heir to that tradition, pursued a universal language and a calculus of reasoning that would settle disputes by computation. The same ambition animated George Boole’s algebraic logic, Gottlob Frege’s formalisation of quantification, and the 1930s analyses of effective calculability by Alonzo Church and Alan Turing. When John McCarthy and his colleagues coined “artificial intelligence” in 1955–56, they were extending a project whose roots reach back to the Renaissance classroom.

Today’s AI systems — large language models that draft arguments, planning agents that select actions — once again force Erasmus’s questions upon us. Do these systems exercise anything resembling judgement or free will? What becomes of human responsibility when reasoning can be automated at scale? The birthday of the Rotterdam humanist is a reminder that artificial intelligence is not merely a technical achievement; it is the latest turn in a long conversation about the nature and limits of the thinking mind.
