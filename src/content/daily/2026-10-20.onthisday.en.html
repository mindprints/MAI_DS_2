1859: John Dewey is born

On 20 October 1859, in Burlington, Vermont, John Dewey was born — a philosopher, psychologist, and educational reformer who would become one of America's leading pragmatists. He is rarely mentioned in histories of artificial intelligence, which tend to favour logicians and engineers, yet a thread runs from his ideas straight into debates that still animate AI research today.

Dewey's central claim, developed across decades but crystallised in his 1896 essay "The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology," was that mind and knowledge are not static mirrors of an external world. Instead, they emerge from an ongoing loop of action and consequence: an organism acts, the environment responds, and understanding is built through that interaction. He rejected what he called the "spectator theory of knowledge" — the idea that cognition is passive observation followed by internal representation. Knowing, for Dewey, is something you do.

This emphasis on learning-by-doing fed into twentieth-century psychology, including the behaviourist tradition that studied trial-and-error learning in animals and, later, machines. It resurfaces, often unacknowledged, in two major strands of AI:

  • Reinforcement learning, where agents improve through reward signals generated by acting in an environment rather than by absorbing pre-digested facts;
  • Embodied and situated robotics, associated with figures like Rodney Brooks, who argued in the 1980s that intelligent behaviour could arise from an agent's interaction with the world without requiring an explicit internal model — a very Deweyan rejection of the spectator stance.

Today's systems — robots learning to grasp objects through millions of trial attempts, game-playing agents refining strategy through self-play, even chatbots fine-tuned by human feedback loops — all quietly inherit Dewey's conviction that intelligence is forged in action, not assembled from static knowledge alone. The Museum of Artificial Intelligence remembers him not as a technologist, but as one of the thinkers who helped shape the very question of what it means to know.
