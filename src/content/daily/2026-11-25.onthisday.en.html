1915: Einstein presents the field equations of general relativity

On 25 November 1915, Albert Einstein stood before the Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin and presented the final form of his field equations. These ten interlocking partial differential equations described how matter and energy curve spacetime, giving birth to the modern theory of general relativity. After years of struggle, the equations elegantly unified gravity with the geometry of the universe.

At first glance, curved spacetime seems far removed from artificial intelligence. Yet the intellectual path is surprisingly direct. The equations are notoriously difficult to solve. Exact solutions exist only for highly symmetric cases, such as Schwarzschild's black hole or Friedmann's expanding universe. For realistic astrophysical situations, scientists had no choice but to turn to numerical approximation—work that demanded ever more powerful calculating machines.

In the 1950s and 1960s, pioneering numerical relativists used early computers to evolve Einstein's equations forward in time. Their techniques for handling nonlinear systems, adaptive meshes, and stability constraints fed into the broader development of scientific computing. The same numerical culture later nurtured finite-element methods, computational fluid dynamics, and large-scale simulation—fields that became training grounds for many of the algorithms now central to machine learning.

Today the loop has closed. Physics-informed neural networks embed Einstein's equations (or their simplified cousins) directly into the loss function of a deep network, letting AI discover solutions that classical solvers struggle to reach. Machine-learning pipelines sift gravitational-wave signals from LIGO and Virgo, and convolutional networks help reconstruct the first images of black holes. What began as a pure mathematical description of gravity now supplies both the data and the differential constraints that modern AI systems learn from.

Thus a single lecture in wartime Berlin continues to shape the tools we use to understand the cosmos—and the intelligent machines we build to help us do so.
