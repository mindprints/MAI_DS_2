1969: Warren McCulloch dies

On 24 September 1969 the American neurophysiologist and cybernetician Warren Sturgis McCulloch died in New Haven, Connecticut, at the age of seventy. Born in 1898, he had trained first as a psychologist and physician before turning his restless curiosity to a single, audacious question: how does a brain compute?

The answer he offered, together with the young logician Walter Pitts, appeared in 1943 in the slim but revolutionary paper “A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity.” McCulloch and Pitts showed that a network of greatly simplified neurons—each either firing or silent—could embody the full power of propositional logic. Any function that a Turing machine could compute could, in principle, be realized by such a net. The McCulloch–Pitts neuron became the conceptual ancestor of every artificial neural network that followed.

At the time, electronic digital computers were still laboratory curiosities and the notion of “thinking machines” belonged mainly to science fiction. Yet the paper forged a durable bridge between the binary logic explored by Shannon and Turing and the living wetware of biology. McCulloch later joined the Research Laboratory of Electronics at MIT, moved in the same circles as Norbert Wiener, and mentored many of the researchers who would shape early artificial intelligence and cognitive science.

Although enthusiasm for neural networks waned during the AI winters of the 1970s and late 1980s, the core insight never vanished. When multilayer networks and backpropagation revived connectionism, and when deep learning erupted after 2012, scientists were still building on the foundation laid in 1943: the idea that intelligent behaviour can emerge from the collective activity of simple, densely interconnected units.

Today’s large language models and vision transformers are distant descendants of that logical calculus. Every time a neural network recognises a face or composes a sentence, it quietly echoes the abstract neurons McCulloch imagined. His death on this day closed a singular life; the intellectual current he helped set in motion continues to drive the artificial intelligence of the twenty-first century.
