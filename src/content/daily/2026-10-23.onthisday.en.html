1817: Pierre Larousse is born

On 23 October 1817, Pierre Larousse was born in the small Burgundian town of Toucy, France. Trained as a schoolteacher, he became possessed by a single idea: that all human knowledge could be collected, defined, cross-referenced, and made accessible to anyone who could read. The result, decades later, was the Grand Dictionnaire Universel du XIXe siècle (1866–1876), a fifteen-volume encyclopedic dictionary whose preface declared his ambition "to be a universal teaching power."

That ambition is older than Larousse and far outlived him. It stretches back to Diderot and d'Alembert's Encyclopédie and forward into the twentieth century, every time someone tried to turn the messy sprawl of human knowledge into a structured, machine-tractable form. Larousse's innovation was more practical than philosophical: definitions that pointed to other definitions, a web of cross-references readers could follow associatively rather than only alphabetically — a paper hypertext, in effect.

That same impulse — represent concepts, link them, make the links computable — resurfaced directly in the twentieth-century history of AI:

  • Margaret Masterman and the Cambridge Language Research Unit built thesauri for machine translation in the 1950s.
  • WordNet, built at Princeton from 1985 under George Miller, organized English words into linked semantic networks.
  • Cyc, Doug Lenat's 1984 project, aimed to hand-encode millions of commonsense facts.
  • Today's knowledge graphs power search engines and virtual assistants.

Each of these projects wrestled with the Larousse problem: knowledge never sits still long enough to be fully catalogued, and any single edition is obsolete the moment it's printed. Today's large language models sidestep that problem by ingesting vast, ever-changing troves of digitized text — often, quite literally, descendants of Larousse's own dictionaries — rather than relying on an editor's hand-built structure.

The trade-off hasn't disappeared, only shifted. Larousse's dictionary was authoritative but frozen in time, shaped by one editor's judgment; today's models are current and vast, but their "knowledge" is statistical, unglossed, and harder to audit. The nineteenth-century dream of a single universal reference work has splintered into competing, overlapping systems — yet the underlying wish, to hold all human knowledge in one accessible place, is still what we're chasing every time we ask a chatbot a question.
