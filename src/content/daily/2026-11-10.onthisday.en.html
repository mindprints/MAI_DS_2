1619: René Descartes dreams the foundations of modern thought

On the night of 10 November 1619, twenty-three-year-old René Descartes, a French soldier wintering in Neuburg an der Donau, shut himself in a heated room to escape the cold. There he experienced three intense dreams that he later described as a divine revelation pointing him toward a completely new way of seeking knowledge. He awoke convinced that the path to certainty lay in radical doubt, clear and distinct ideas, and the rigorous application of mathematical method to every domain of thought.

Those dreams became the emotional and intellectual origin of the project that produced the Discourse on the Method (1637), the Meditations on First Philosophy, and the invention of analytic geometry. By uniting algebra with geometry, Descartes gave later centuries a coordinate system in which space itself could be treated as numbers—an indispensable foundation for everything from computer graphics to the vector spaces of modern machine learning. More profoundly, his insistence that the mind is a thinking substance whose operations can be examined with the same clarity as geometric proofs helped launch the long conversation about whether thought itself might be mechanical.

The mechanical philosophy that grew from Cartesian roots treated animals as elaborate automata and raised the question of where, if anywhere, human reason differed. That question travelled through the centuries via Leibniz’s calculating machines, La Mettrie’s L’Homme Machine, the cybernetics of Wiener, and the symbolic systems of early AI. Even the famous “ghost in the machine” critique of dualism kept Descartes at the centre of debates about consciousness and embodiment.

Today large language models and embodied robots force us to revisit the same night of 1619. Can a system that manipulates symbols according to rules truly understand? Does intelligence require the kind of unified, doubting subject Descartes placed at the foundation of knowledge? The dreams that began on a cold Bavarian night still supply the conceptual vocabulary—method, certainty, mind as mechanism—with which we argue about artificial minds. Every time an AI system is asked to explain its reasoning or is tested for genuine comprehension, we are, in a distant but real sense, still waking from Descartes’ three dreams.
