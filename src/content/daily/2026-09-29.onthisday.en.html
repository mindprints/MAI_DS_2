1901: Enrico Fermi is born

On 29 September 1901, Enrico Fermi was born in Rome. One of the towering physicists of the twentieth century, he shaped quantum theory, nuclear physics and statistical mechanics. Although he never worked on artificial intelligence, the mathematical tools and computational habits he helped establish became part of the deep foundation on which modern AI rests.

Fermi-Dirac statistics described how certain particles fill energy states. More immediately useful to later computing, Fermi was an early and energetic user of electronic machines. At Los Alamos he pushed numerical experiments far beyond what hand calculation could achieve, treating the computer as a new kind of laboratory instrument.

With Stanisław Ulam he helped launch the Monte Carlo method—solving stubborn physical problems by intelligent random sampling. The 1955 Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou simulation, one of the first large computer experiments on a nonlinear system, produced startling quasi-periodic behaviour and helped open the fields of chaos theory and computational physics.

Those same ideas reappear throughout contemporary AI. Monte Carlo tree search guided AlphaGo; Markov-chain Monte Carlo sampling powers Bayesian machine learning; stochastic simulation underpins countless reinforcement-learning and generative models. Even the casual “Fermi estimate”—an order-of-magnitude guess made with scant data—mirrors the approximate reasoning AI systems perform every day.

When a large language model draws its next token from a probability distribution, or when a robot plans by running thousands of simulated futures, it is working in a tradition that runs through Fermi’s statistical physics and his early trust in machine-assisted discovery. His birthday is a quiet reminder that artificial intelligence did not spring from nowhere; it grew from the patient marriage of mathematics, physics and the first electronic computers.
