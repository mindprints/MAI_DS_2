1894: Norbert Wiener is born

On 26 November 1894, Norbert Wiener was born in Columbia, Missouri. A child prodigy who earned his PhD from Harvard at eighteen, Wiener became one of the twentieth century’s most restless mathematical minds. His wartime work on anti-aircraft predictors and his later synthesis of communication, control and feedback crystallized into the new field he named cybernetics.

In Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine (1948), Wiener argued that purposeful behavior in living organisms and engineered systems could be understood through the same mathematical language of information, noise and circular causation. The book rapidly crossed disciplinary borders, influencing early neural-network research, operations research, and the first generation of AI pioneers who met at Dartmouth and elsewhere.

Wiener’s insistence that machines and minds both traffic in signals helped legitimize the idea that intelligence itself might be studied as an information process. Concepts he popularized—negative feedback, homeostasis, the man–machine system—reappear today in reinforcement learning, adaptive control, and the architecture of large-scale autonomous agents.

He was also an early and vocal critic of the social risks of automation, warning that powerful predictive systems could concentrate power and erode human agency. Those cautions still echo in contemporary debates about algorithmic accountability and the governance of AI.

From a single birthday in 1894 stretches a clear intellectual thread: the cybernetic vision of feedback and information remains woven into the conceptual fabric of artificial intelligence more than a century later.
