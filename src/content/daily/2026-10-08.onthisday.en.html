1920: Frank Herbert is born

On 8 October 1920, Frank Herbert was born in Tacoma, Washington. Though best known as a science-fiction novelist, Herbert's masterpiece Dune (1965) and its sequels have left a lasting mark on how societies imagine artificial intelligence and the consequences of creating minds that are not human.

In the distant future of Dune, humanity has endured the Butlerian Jihad—a crusade against “thinking machines.” The resulting commandment, “Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind,” shapes an entire civilization. Mentats, human beings trained to perform feats of computation and analysis, replace computers. The novels thereby explore themes that remain central to AI: the nature of intelligence, the risks of autonomous systems, and the value of human judgment.

Herbert did not write technical papers on logic or neural nets. Yet his fiction travelled into the laboratories and lecture halls where AI was being invented. Researchers and ethicists have repeatedly cited the Butlerian Jihad when discussing control problems, value alignment, and the social desirability of human-level AI. The books made vivid a set of questions that formal philosophy and computer science later tried to answer with greater precision.

Science fiction has long served as a laboratory for ideas that later become engineering problems. Mary Shelley’s creature, Karel Čapek’s robots, and Isaac Asimov’s laws all prepared the cultural ground. Herbert’s contribution was to imagine not merely the machine, but an entire ecology of politics, ecology, and religion organized around the rejection of machine intelligence. That imaginative act still colors public debate.

Today, as large language models and autonomous agents enter everyday life, the questions Herbert dramatized feel newly urgent. Should we build systems that resemble human minds? How do we keep them under meaningful human control? The anniversary of his birth is a reminder that the intellectual history of AI includes not only theorems and transistors but also the stories that taught us to worry—and to wonder—about thinking machines.
