1898: Warren McCulloch is born

On 16 November 1898, Warren Sturgis McCulloch was born in Orange, New Jersey. A psychiatrist, neurophysiologist and cybernetician, McCulloch would become one of the key figures whose ideas helped lay the foundations for artificial neural networks and, ultimately, much of modern artificial intelligence.

After studying mathematics and philosophy, and later medicine, McCulloch became fascinated by the problem of how the brain could produce complex thoughts from simple cellular components. In the early 1940s he began collaborating with the brilliant young logician Walter Pitts. Together they published their landmark 1943 paper, “A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity.”

In that paper they showed that networks of simplified neurons—each either firing or not, like a logic gate—could in principle compute any logical or arithmetic function. This was the first mathematical model of a neural network, and it demonstrated that the brain could be understood as a kind of computer. The work directly inspired John von Neumann’s thinking about reliable computation from unreliable components and became a cornerstone of the emerging field of cybernetics, alongside Norbert Wiener’s ideas on feedback and control.

Although the early optimism about neural nets faded during the “AI winter,” McCulloch and Pitts’s insight never disappeared. It resurfaced in the perceptron of the 1950s, the backpropagation algorithms of the 1980s, and the deep learning revolution of the 2010s. Today’s large language models and convolutional networks are distant but direct descendants of that 1943 logical calculus.

McCulloch spent his later career at MIT, continuing to explore the relationship between mind, brain and machine until his death in 1969. On this day we remember the birth of a thinker who helped turn the mystery of thought into a problem that engineers and computer scientists could attack—and who thereby helped make artificial intelligence possible.
