1973: A Court Rules Who Invented the Computer

On 19 October 1973, in a Minneapolis courtroom, Judge Earl R. Larson handed down a decision that quietly rewrote the official history of computing. Ruling in the case of Honeywell v. Sperry Rand, he declared the foundational patent behind ENIAC — long celebrated as the first general-purpose electronic computer — invalid, because its central ideas had been derived from the work of another man entirely.

That man was John Vincent Atanasoff, a physics professor at Iowa State College. Between 1937 and 1942, with graduate student Clifford Berry, he built a machine to solve systems of linear equations. The Atanasoff-Berry Computer (ABC) never worked perfectly and was never patented, but it introduced ideas that would define electronic computing:

  • binary rather than decimal arithmetic;
  • logic performed by electronic vacuum-tube switches instead of mechanical gears;
  • memory that regenerated itself using capacitors, an ancestor of modern RAM.

In 1941, John Mauchly, a physicist with his own computing ambitions, visited Atanasoff in Iowa, examined the ABC closely, and corresponded with him about its design. Soon after, Mauchly moved to the University of Pennsylvania, where he and J. Presper Eckert built ENIAC, completed in 1945 and patented in 1964. The patent eventually passed to Sperry Rand, which collected licensing fees from computer manufacturers across the industry — until Honeywell refused to pay and sued. The resulting trial, one of the longest in U.S. patent history, forced engineers, lawyers, and historians to reconstruct, line by line, how the idea of an electronic digital computer actually came into being.

Larson's verdict was unambiguous: ENIAC's inventors "did not themselves first invent the automatic electronic digital computer, but instead derived that subject matter from one Dr. John Vincent Atanasoff." The ruling did not change what had already been built, but it changed the story — restoring a quiet, underfunded experiment in an Iowa basement to its rightful place in the lineage of machines that would eventually run neural networks, search engines, and language models.

It is also, in its way, an early preview of disputes that feel very contemporary: who truly originates an idea in computing, and who merely builds the visible, celebrated version of it. Every large language model and robotic system today sits atop a tower of such contested, layered inventions — a reminder that the history of AI is also a history of credit, memory, and who gets remembered first.
